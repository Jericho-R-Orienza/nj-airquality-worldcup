Summary of Findings

Three Things the Data Shows

Shore roads spike. Corridor roads don't.

Jersey Shore highway approach roads average +39% higher traffic in summer vs. winter, with one barrier island road reaching +119%. The MetLife Stadium transit corridor, covering Bergen, Hudson, and Passaic counties, shows essentially no seasonal volume change (+0.1%). The two zones face categorically different risk profiles.

Summer ozone is 82% higher before a single fan arrives.

Statewide summer ozone AQI averages 47.6 vs. 28.2 on NFL fall game days, representing a 69% gap driven entirely by heat, solar intensity, and photochemical reactions. Every World Cup match adds event-day vehicle pressure on top of this pre-existing seasonal burden.

NFL game days leave no detectable AQI fingerprint.

A well-managed transit event at MetLife does not produce a measurable county-level air quality spike. The risk from the World Cup is not any single game. It is 8 games across 5 weeks, with 4 on weekdays overlapping rush hour, and transit capped at 40,000 fans while the stadium holds 82,500.

Finding 01

The Traffic Story: Two Very Different Geographies

Not all NJ roads follow the same seasonal pattern. Highway monitoring data from the Federal Highway Administration's Travel Monitoring Analysis System (TMAS), comprising 15 months of hourly vehicle counts across 181 permanent stations, reveals a sharp geographic divide between the MetLife transit corridor and the Jersey Shore.

Summer vs. winter Average Daily Traffic at 17 curated TMAS monitoring stations. Corridor stations are essentially flat; Shore stations spike sharply.

The corridor feeding MetLife is flat because it operates at or near capacity year-round and has no seasonal slack to absorb. The Shore spikes because it is a leisure-destination road network, compressed between ocean and development, that doubles in function during summer. The World Cup introduces stadium-scale event pressure into the same months when the Shore corridor is already at its annual ceiling.

Side-by-side comparison of summer vs. winter change in traffic (left) and ozone AQI (right) by zone. Transit Corridor ozone rises sharply despite flat traffic, confirming the chemistry-driven mechanism.

Traffic volume percent change (summer vs. winter) at individual TMAS stations. Dashed lines indicate zone-level PM2.5 AQI increases for reference.

Finding 02

The Air Quality Baseline: Summer Is Already a Stressed System

EPA Air Quality System data for 2023–2025, comprising PM2.5, ozone, and NO₂ readings from 28 monitoring stations across NJ, establishes a clear picture: summer air quality is measurably worse than winter in every county and every pollutant, and it deteriorates for reasons that have nothing to do with tourism. Adding World Cup traffic pressure compounds a system that is already operating in a degraded state.

Monthly average PM2.5 AQI by zone across all 12 months (2023–2025 combined). The June 2023 Canadian wildfire event is annotated. Summer spike is consistent across years independent of the wildfire.

Summer (June–August) vs. winter (November–December) average PM2.5 AQI by zone. Jersey Shore shows the largest relative increase at +66%.

A note on NO₂: summer nitrogen dioxide levels are slightly lower than winter at several corridor stations, the opposite of what a simple "more traffic = more pollution" argument would predict. This is chemically expected. Warmer temperatures accelerate the conversion of NO₂ into ground-level ozone, consuming NO₂ before it accumulates at the monitor. Summer NO₂ suppression is not evidence of cleaner air; it is evidence that the ozone-formation reaction is running faster.

Jersey Shore average daily traffic (left axis) and ozone AQI (right axis) by month. Both datasets peak together in summer, confirming that in infrastructure-constrained coastal zones, traffic volume and air quality move in lockstep.

Finding 03

Three Natural Experiments Already in the Data

The World Cup has not happened yet, but the data does not require projection. Three historical conditions already embedded in the 2023–2025 dataset answer different parts of the air quality question directly: NFL game days at MetLife, Memorial Day weekend, and 4th of July weekend. Each one is a natural experiment with a known treatment and a measurable outcome.

NFL game day average AQI vs. matched control Sundays (Bergen, Hudson, Passaic counties) across 34 game dates from the 2023 and 2024 seasons. Difference is within statistical noise for all three pollutants.

This is the most important baseline in the analysis. A well-managed transit event at MetLife, drawing 70,000 fans through the same corridor and infrastructure, does not register as a detectable air quality signal at county-level monitoring stations. The transit system can absorb a single large event. This is the argument's foundation, not its conclusion: the World Cup is not a stadium event. It is a sustained regional stressor across eight weeks, four of which land on weekday evenings during rush hour, with transit capped well below demand.

Shore road traffic on July 4th weekend vs. the average of all other July weekends, by day of week (2023–2025). July 4th volumes are at or below the typical July Saturday baseline because July in NJ is already at ceiling.

The July 4th finding is not a null result; it is a capacity argument. Shore roads do not spike on the holiday weekend because they are already running at their seasonal peak every July weekend. The World Cup Final on July 19 lands into this same saturated baseline. There is no slack left in the system to absorb an event-day surge.

Jersey Shore ozone AQI on Memorial Day weekend vs. non-holiday May weekend baseline, by year and day of week. 2024 Friday shows +130% above baseline. Three-year pattern: when traffic rises above baseline, ozone follows; when it doesn't (2025), ozone doesn't either.

In 2024, Memorial Day Friday on the Jersey Shore saw Shore traffic 26% above the May baseline and ozone AQI 130% above the May baseline on the same day. In 2025, when travel demand fell below baseline due to weather suppression, ozone fell below baseline too. Across three years the directional relationship is consistent: traffic pressure and ozone move together on the Shore. This is the same relationship that will play out across eight World Cup matchdays in June and July, in the same geography and the same season.

The Argument

Why the World Cup Is Different

NJ Transit has committed to moving 40,000 fans per matchday by rail, 20,000 more than any previous MetLife event. The stadium holds 82,500. The gap of roughly 42,500 fans per match has no guaranteed transit access. FIFA's own COO publicly warned that elevated fares push fans toward cars, and MetLife has been designated a no-parking venue, meaning fans who drive disperse into surrounding municipalities such as Rutherford, Carlstadt, and North Bergen, rather than concentrating in a single lot. Four of the eight matches fall on weekdays; two of those overlap directly with the evening rush hour.

The communities absorbing this pressure are not the ones receiving the benefit. The transit corridor from Penn Station through Secaucus Junction to East Rutherford runs directly past the Hackensack River Superfund site and through municipalities including Kearny, Harrison, and Newark, which have some of the highest asthma rates and most industrially burdened air sheds in the state. Displaced commuters rerouted through Newark Penn Station on blackout-window matchdays add unplanned vehicle trips on top of event traffic in a city that already sits beneath major highway interchanges, flight paths from Newark Liberty Airport, and Port Newark freight operations.

The risk is not a single catastrophic day. It is eight weeks of compounding pressure on a system with no remaining margin: stadium traffic, Shore tourism, weekday commuter displacement, and peak summer ozone chemistry, all active simultaneously in a geography that carries a disproportionate existing pollution burden.

Central Thesis

"NJ's summer air quality baseline is already measurably elevated above winter. A single NFL game at MetLife produces no detectable additional AQI spike, suggesting the infrastructure can absorb a normal large event. The World Cup is not a normal large event. The risk is not a single catastrophic day. It is eight weeks of compounding pressure on a system with no remaining margin."

Environmental Justice Finding

Essex County, which includes Newark, one of the most pollution-burdened cities in New Jersey, has no PM2.5, NO₂, or ozone monitoring station in the federal EPA AQS dataset for the 2023–2025 period. The communities most likely to absorb the environmental cost of the World Cup are the ones for which we have the least data. That monitoring gap is itself a finding, and it points directly to where temporary sensor deployment is most needed.

Recommendations

What the Data Suggests

These recommendations are ordered by impact and by alignment with NJDEP's existing regulatory authority.

01

Transit Fare Parity on Matchdays

Subsidizing NJ Transit matchday fares to be competitive with the cost of driving is the single most evidence-supported intervention. FIFA's own COO predicted car spillover directly from elevated fares. London's 2012 Olympics used a flat-fare transit policy on event days and demonstrably shifted trips from road to rail. The cost of the subsidy is substantially lower than the cumulative environmental cost of the alternative.

02

Temporary Air Quality Sensor Deployment

Essex County has no federal AQ monitor for 2023–2025. Deploying low-cost sensors, such as PurpleAir units, in Essex and Hudson County transit corridors for the duration of the tournament would close that gap in real time. Sensor placement at Secaucus Junction, Harrison Fan Hub, and Kearny would generate matchday-vs-baseline data that does not currently exist and would produce an evidence base for managing future large events at MetLife.

03

Diesel Idle Enforcement at Shuttle Staging Areas

New Jersey anti-idling regulations exist but are rarely enforced at large events. Diesel shuttle buses staging at Secaucus Junction and Harrison Fan Hub for hours before and after each match represent a concentrated, localized emission source adjacent to communities with existing respiratory health disparities. Designated enforcement zones on matchdays require no new infrastructure, only the application of existing regulatory authority.

04

Prioritize Electric Shuttle Buses for Matchday Routes

NJ Transit is actively converting its bus fleet to battery-electric. Allocating EV buses specifically to the eight World Cup matchday shuttle routes eliminates the diesel idle signature at the two highest-burden transit nodes identified in this analysis. This is a logistics decision, not a capital expenditure.

05

Long-Term Investment in Statewide NJ Transit Expansion

The World Cup exposes a structural problem that predates it: NJ Transit's coverage gaps force car dependency on residents who would otherwise choose transit. Sustained capital investment in rail and bus rapid transit expansion, closing coverage gaps in underserved Essex, Union, and Middlesex corridors, would reduce baseline vehicle volume across the state every day, not just on matchdays. Affordable, frequent, and geographically broader transit service is the only intervention that meaningfully lowers the daily driver count rather than managing its consequences. Every dollar invested in network expansion multiplies across years of reduced vehicle miles traveled, lower NOₓ and PM2.5 emissions, and reduced cumulative ozone precursor load, in the same communities this analysis identifies as already bearing a disproportionate air quality burden.
